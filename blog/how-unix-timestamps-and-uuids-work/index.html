Unix Timestamps and UUIDs Explained for Beginners

What a Unix timestamp is, how it converts to a date, what a UUID is and when developers use them, with free converters.

Developers use two kinds of identifiers all the time: timestamps, to record when something happened, and UUIDs, to give things unique names.

What is a Unix timestamp?

A Unix timestamp is the number of seconds that have passed since 1 January 1970 at 00:00 UTC. It is one simple number that every computer in the world can compare, whatever the time zone. A 10-digit value is usually in seconds and a 13-digit value is in milliseconds.

How to convert one

  1. Open the Timestamp Converter.
  2. Paste the number to see the date in your local time and in UTC.
  3. Or pick a date to get its timestamp.

Good habits

  • Store times as UTC timestamps and convert them to local time only for display.
  • Check whether your system uses seconds or milliseconds.
  • Remember that the same timestamp shows different clock times in different time zones.

What is a UUID?

A UUID is a 128-bit identifier written like 3f2b8c1e-9a4d-4e6f-8b21-5c7d9e0a1f34. Version 4 UUIDs are made from random numbers. The chance of two being the same is so small that they are treated as unique, which is why databases and APIs use them as IDs without checking with a central server.

Using the UUID Generator

Choose how many you need and copy them. Use them as record IDs, test data or request IDs. A UUID is an identifier, not a secret, so do not use it as a password.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a timestamp start in 1970?

It is a convention chosen by early Unix systems, called the Unix epoch.

Can two UUIDs ever match?

In theory yes, but the probability is so tiny it is ignored in practice.
